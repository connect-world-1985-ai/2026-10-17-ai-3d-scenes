Part II — Fundamentals

Meshes, PBR materials, light and tone mapping

Why does one agent-made scene look like a plastic toy and another like a photograph? Rarely because of more triangles. Mostly because of five things: physically based materials, light that comes from an environment rather than a single lamp, believable shadows, correct colour handling, and tone mapping. Each is a few lines of code, which is why agents can get them right — and why they often forget one.

Meshes: geometry + material

A mesh = geometry (vertex positions, normals, texture coordinates (UVs), triangle indices) + material (how the surface reacts to light). In Three.js: new THREE.Mesh(new THREE.BoxGeometry(1,1,1), new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({color: 0x888888})).

  • Normals are per-vertex directions used for lighting. Flat versus smooth shading is only a question of whether neighbouring triangles share normals.
  • UVs map a 2D image onto the surface. Procedural boxes have simple UVs; generated meshes (page 10) often have messy ones.
  • Real assets usually arrive as glTF/GLB (the "JPEG of 3D", ISO/IEC 12113:2022), which carries geometry, PBR materials, textures and animation in one file. Verified

Physically based rendering (PBR)

PBR materials describe a surface with a few physical parameters instead of artistic hacks. The renderer then computes how light bounces off them using approximations of real optics (microfacet theory). The two that matter most:

  • Metalness (0 or 1, rarely between). Metals have no diffuse colour; their reflections are tinted. Non-metals (dielectrics) have a diffuse colour plus a weak, white reflection of about 4% at normal incidence.
  • Roughness (0–1): how blurry reflections are. Roughness 0 is a mirror; 1 is chalk.

Plus base colour (albedo), normal maps (fake small bumps), ambient occlusion, emissive (glowing windows, neon). MeshPhysicalMaterial adds clearcoat (car paint), transmission (glass), sheen (cloth) and iridescence.

The Fresnel effect (Schlick's approximation) is why PBR looks real: every surface becomes more reflective at grazing angles. It is why a lake mirrors the sky near the horizon but shows the bottom at your feet, and why oceans look right only when Fresnel is in the shader.

Light: direct and image-based

Real-time engines combine two kinds of light Practice:

  • Direct lights: directional (the sun, parallel rays), point (a bulb), spot (a street lamp), plus hemisphere (sky above, ground below) as a cheap ambient. Each casts shadows only if you ask and pay for it.
  • Image-based lighting (IBL): a 360° picture of the surroundings (an HDRI or environment map) lights every surface from every direction. Without it, metals reflect nothing and look black. Three.js pre-filters the map with PMREMGenerator so rough surfaces sample a blurrier version. Set scene.environment = texture and every standard material uses it.

Free HDRIs come from Poly Haven (CC0). Without any file, Three.js's RoomEnvironment or a rendered sky (page 07) works as an environment. Verified One of the strongest visual upgrades an agent can make is adding an environment map. Inference

Shadows

Real-time shadows are mostly shadow maps: render the scene once from the light's point of view, store depth, then for each pixel ask "is something closer to the light than me?".

  • Resolution versus coverage. One 2048² map stretched over a whole city gives blocky shadows. Fix it by keeping the light's shadow camera box tight, or with cascaded shadow maps: several maps at different distances. Three.js r186 added a SunLight with cascades in the WebGPU path. Verified
  • Acne and peter-panning. Striped self-shadowing ("acne") is fixed with a small negative shadow.bias. Too much bias detaches shadows from objects ("peter-panning").
  • Soft shadows. In r186 PCFSoftShadowMap was removed; PCFShadowMap is now soft. Agents trained on older code still emit the removed constant and get a console warning. Verified (seen while building this guide)
  • Contact shadows and ambient occlusion (SSAO, GTAO) darken creases where ambient light cannot reach. They are cheap realism for cities.

Colour management and tone mapping

Lighting maths happens in linear light with values that can exceed 1 (the sun is far brighter than a white wall). Screens show 0–1 in a non-linear sRGB encoding. Two steps bridge that gap:

  1. Colour space. Colour textures (albedo) are sRGB and must be tagged texture.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace; data textures (normal, roughness) stay linear. Output is sRGB by default since r152. Mistagging gives washed-out or too-dark results, one of the most common agent bugs. Verified
  2. Tone mapping compresses HDR to the screen range, like a camera does. Three.js offers None, Linear, Reinhard, Cineon, ACES Filmic, AgX and Neutral; the default is none. Verified
    • ACES Filmic: punchy and filmic. Three.js's version contains a built-in 1/0.6 exposure boost, which is why official demos use tiny exposures such as 0.1. Verified
    • AgX (from Blender, Blender's default since 4.0): more natural highlight roll-off; bright colours go towards white like a real camera.
    • Khronos PBR Neutral (2024): faithful product colours, little drama.
    Pick the operator, then adjust toneMappingExposure.

The "make it look real" checklist (in order of payoff)
  1. Environment map (HDRI or rendered sky) for every PBR material.
  2. Tone mapping on (ACES or AgX), exposure tuned.
  3. Correct colour spaces on textures.
  4. One shadow-casting sun with a tight shadow box.
  5. Fog that matches the sky colour.
  6. Restrained bloom on emissive lights.
  7. Some roughness variation: nothing in the real world is uniformly smooth.
Inference, distilled from the official Three.js examples and the failure lists on page 11.
