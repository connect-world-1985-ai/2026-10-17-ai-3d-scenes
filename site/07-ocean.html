Part III — Recipes

Recipe: ocean with sky, sun and reflections

"A realistic ocean at sunset" is one of the most common prompts in AI-3D demos, because the payoff is huge for little code: a sun glitter path, a horizon that melts into the sky, waves that catch the light. Under the hood there is a clear ladder, from a flat blue plane to film-grade FFT spectra. Most agent demos stop at rung 2 or 3, and that is usually enough.

Anatomy of an ocean picture

An ocean is mostly reflected sky. Five ingredients make it convincing Practice:

  1. Wave shape: the surface moves (vertex displacement) and has small ripples (normal maps).
  2. Fresnel: near the horizon the water is a mirror; at your feet it is dark and deep-coloured.
  3. Sky and sun: something worth reflecting, plus a bright specular highlight that becomes a glitter path.
  4. Subsurface colour: light passing through thin wave crests turns them turquoise.
  5. Foam where waves fold over, and a horizon that blends with fog or atmosphere.

Wave models

Sum of sines

Add a few sine waves with different directions, wavelengths and speeds to a flat grid in the vertex shader. Smooth and rolling, but crests are as round as troughs, which reads as "jelly". Mark Finch's chapter in GPU Gems 1, ch. 1 (used in Uru: Ages Beyond Myst) is the classic reference. Verified

Gerstner (trochoidal) waves

Move vertices horizontally towards the crests as well as up and down. Crests sharpen and troughs widen, just like real deep-water waves. A steepness parameter Q controls it: 0 gives sines; Q = 1/(kA) per wave gives sharp crests; higher values make the surface loop over itself (the same chapter). Verified Unreal's built-in Water system uses Gerstner waves. Verified (UE 4.27 docs)

// one Gerstner wave: direction D (unit 2D), wavenumber k = 2π/L, amplitude A, steepness Q
float phase = k * dot(D, p.xz) - sqrt(9.81 * k) * time;   // deep-water dispersion ω = √(g k)
p.xz += Q * A * D * cos(phase);
p.y  +=     A     * sin(phase);

The sqrt(9.81 * k) term (deep-water dispersion) makes long waves travel faster than short ones. That one line is why a few Gerstner waves look like sea rather than a screensaver.

FFT ocean (Tessendorf)

Instead of a handful of waves, use hundreds of thousands. Generate random wave amplitudes that follow a measured ocean spectrum (Phillips or JONSWAP), then sum them all at once with an inverse Fast Fourier Transform on a tiling grid each frame. That is J. Tessendorf's "Simulating Ocean Water", used across film and games. Sea of Thieves uses this FFT technique, stylised (SIGGRAPH 2018), not Gerstner as often assumed. Verified

  • Cascades: 3–4 FFT tiles at different scales hide visible repetition. NVIDIA uses 4. The single-file WebGL demo abyssal-ocean (MIT, 55 stars) uses 3 tiles of 768 m, 121 m and 19 m. Verified (README)
  • Jacobian foam: where the displaced surface folds over itself (the Jacobian determinant drops below a threshold of about 0.3–0.5), waves are breaking. Paint foam there and let it fade over time (NVIDIA CGDC 2015 slides). Verified
  • The best explanations are videos: Acerola's "I Tried Simulating The Entire Ocean", where a sum of waves hit its limit at ~650 waves while the FFT version showed 4.2 million waves at 150 fps on his GPU (transcript), and Jump Trajectory's FFT ocean video with its Unity code (MIT, 808 stars, "a prototype"). Verified

Shading the water

  • Reflection. Cheapest: look up the sky colour in the reflected direction. That alone gives most of the realism in open sea. Planar reflection (render the scene again, mirrored: Three.js Reflector and Water) is exact for flat water but doubles the render cost. Screen-space reflections (SSR) are cheap but lose everything that is off-screen. Practice
  • Fresnel mixes reflection and the deep-water colour by viewing angle (Schlick's approximation). Seascape uses a cheap pow(1 - dot(n, -eye), 3) capped at 0.5. Verified
  • Sun specular: a very sharp highlight; with many small normals it breaks into a glitter path.
  • Subsurface scattering (approximation): add turquoise where crests are high and the sun is behind them. Sea of Thieves blends deep and subsurface colour by view angle, sun direction and a wave-peak mask. Verified
  • Refraction and depth colour matter near shores and in shallow pools: Three.js Water2 (Reflector + Refractor, flow maps).

Typical failures in agent-made oceans
  • Grey band at the horizon: fog colour does not match the sky. Fixed while building the wave toy above by fogging towards the sky function itself.
  • Visible tiling: one normal map repeated. Fix with two maps at different scales and speeds, or distance fade.
  • Jelly waves: sines with no Gerstner horizontal motion.
  • Flat, matte-looking water: no environment map, or no tone mapping.
  • Blown-out sun: ACES with exposure 1. The official example uses exposure 0.1.
  • 404 on waternormals.jpg: the agent copied the official example, but the texture is not on the CDN path it used.

Ready prompt (rung 3, browser)

Build a single index.html (no build step) with Three.js r186 from a CDN import map. Scene: open ocean at golden hour, camera 6 m above the water, slowly orbiting. Water: a 256×256 PlaneGeometry (400 m) with 6 Gerstner waves in a ShaderMaterial vertex shader (deep-water dispersion ω = sqrt(9.81·k), steepness Q ≤ 0.8 total), analytic normals, Schlick Fresnel between deep colour #0a2a3a and the sky colour in the reflected direction, a sharp sun specular, turquoise subsurface tint on crests, foam where the Jacobian < 0.4. Sky: three.js Sky add-on (turbidity 6, rayleigh 2, sun 4° elevation); fog colour = sky colour at the horizon. Renderer: ACESFilmic tone mapping, exposure ~0.5, pixel ratio ≤ 2. Add a small GUI with sliders for sun elevation, wave height and choppiness, and an FPS/draw-call HUD. After writing it, open it in a headless browser, check the console for errors, take screenshots at sun 4° and 30°, and fix anything that looks wrong (horizon band, tiling, black water).
