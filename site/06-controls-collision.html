Part II — Fundamentals

First-person controls and collision

"Walk through the streets" sounds simple, yet it is where agent-made scenes most often break: walking through walls, floating above stairs, getting stuck on a kerb, a mouse that spins wildly. Free walking needs three things: mouse-look, movement relative to where you are looking, and collision against the world. This page shows the standard browser recipe, the same one the official Three.js FPS example uses.

Mouse-look with pointer lock

The Pointer Lock API hides the cursor and delivers raw mouse movement (movementX, movementY), so you can turn without the cursor hitting the screen edge. It must be started by a user click, and Esc releases it. Verified

  • Use rotation order 'YXZ': yaw (turn) around world Y, then pitch (look up and down). Clamp pitch to about ±85°, or the view flips over the top.
  • Movement is relative to the camera's forward direction flattened to the ground (set y to 0 and renormalise). Otherwise looking down makes you walk into the floor.
  • Touch screens have no pointer lock: provide drag-to-look and on-screen buttons. The demo below does both.

Collision: capsule against octree

The player is not a point; it is a capsule (a cylinder with round ends, about 0.35 m radius and 1.7 m tall). Round ends slide smoothly over kerbs and stairs instead of snagging on edges. The world's triangles go into an octree, a spatial index that splits space into nested cubes, so each frame only tests the few triangles near the player.

// the core of the step, as in the official games_fps example
collider.translate(velocity.clone().multiplyScalar(dt));
const hit = worldOctree.capsuleIntersect(collider);
onFloor = false;
if (hit) {
  onFloor = hit.normal.y > 0;
  if (!onFloor) velocity.addScaledVector(hit.normal, -hit.normal.dot(velocity)); // slide along walls
  collider.translate(hit.normal.multiplyScalar(hit.depth));                     // push out
}
camera.position.copy(collider.end);   // eyes at the top of the capsule

Sub-steps matter: at low frame rates one big step can carry the capsule straight through a thin wall ("tunnelling"). Running the physics 5 times per frame with dt/5 fixes it cheaply. Practice (the official example does the same)

Try it: walk a street block

Click the button to lock the pointer. Use WASD or arrows to move, the mouse to look, Space to jump and Esc to release. Climb the stairs in the plaza and walk up the ramp. Then untick collision and walk through a building. Tick show octree to see the spatial index.

What feels "right" in a street walk

  • Scale. Eye height ~1.6–1.7 m, walking speed ~1.4 m/s, running ~3–5 m/s. Many demos move at 10 m/s, which makes streets feel like toys. The night-street project walks at 1.4 m/s and sprints at 3.1 m/s, with head-bob tied to stride. Verified (repo README)
  • Field of view 60–75°; wider exaggerates speed.
  • Acceleration and damping rather than instant start and stop.
  • Step height. Capsules climb small steps naturally; very high steps need ramps or an explicit step-up.
  • Collision proxies. For big cities, collide against simple invisible boxes instead of every detailed triangle.

Physics engines, when you need more

For cars, ragdolls, stacking boxes or many dynamic objects, use a real physics engine compiled to WebAssembly: Rapier (it also has a character controller), Jolt (jolt-physics), or cannon-es. The AA-3D benchmark sandbox ships Rapier for exactly this reason. Verified For walking through a static city, the octree approach above is enough.

Common agent failures

  • Moving the camera directly with no collider, so you walk through walls. Fix: ask for capsule-vs-octree collision explicitly.
  • Building the octree before the meshes are positioned, or from a scene that includes the skybox, so you stand on the sky dome.
  • Calling requestPointerLock() outside a click handler: the browser refuses.
  • Forgetting preventDefault on Space and arrow keys, so the page scrolls while you walk.
  • Headless tests cannot lock the pointer. A good agent adds a keyboard or scripted-path mode so it can test movement itself (see page 11).
