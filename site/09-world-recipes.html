Part III — Recipes

World recipes: terrain, atmosphere, vegetation, crowds, camera

Oceans and cities get the attention, but what makes a scene feel like a place is everything around them: ground that rolls away, air you can see through, a sun that moves, grass that sways, things that move on their own, and a camera that behaves like a person or a drone. Each recipe below has a simple version an agent gets right on the first try, and the upgrades that make the difference.

Terrain

Terrain is a heightfield, y = f(x, z), usually fBm noise (page 04). Two ways to draw it:

  • Displaced mesh: a PlaneGeometry grid with heights set on the CPU or in the vertex shader. Simple, works with normal lighting and shadows. See webgl_geometry_terrain and webgpu_tsl_procedural_terrain. Verified
  • Raymarched heightfield: no mesh, unlimited detail, as in iq's Elevated and Rainforest. Expensive per pixel.

Inigo Quilez's terrain article gives the standard recipe Verified:

  • colour by altitude and slope (the normal's y): grass on flat ground, rock on steep slopes, snow high up;
  • randomise the thresholds with noise;
  • light with a warm sun plus a dimmer bluish sky fill;
  • add distance fog.

Upgrades:

  • Erosion-like noise (ridged or derivative fBm).
  • Texture splatting with tiling PBR textures (rock, grass, dirt) blended by slope; triplanar mapping so cliffs do not stretch.
  • Level of detail (geometry clipmaps or chunked quadtrees) for kilometres of view.
  • Real elevation data (DEM) via CesiumJS or BlenderGIS (route 5).

Fog and atmosphere

  • Distance fog: scene.fog = new THREE.Fog(color, near, far) (linear) or FogExp2(color, density), i.e. exponential extinction exp(-d·density). Nearly free; it also hides the far clip plane and LOD pops. Verified
  • Match the sky. The fog colour must equal the sky colour at the horizon in the view direction, otherwise you get a grey band. That is the single most common fog bug, and the one fixed while building this guide's wave toy. Practice
  • Height fog: denser near the ground, as in valleys and city haze. Official TSL examples: webgpu_fog_height and webgpu_custom_fog.
  • Sun-aware fog: tint fog towards the sun colour when looking at the sun. One extra line, big payoff at golden hour.
  • Volumetric light ("god rays", lamp cones): raymarch through a fog volume in a post pass, or fake it with additive cones. The night-street project uses volumetric lamp cones in its brief. Verified
  • Physical atmosphere (Bruneton, Hillaire) gives correct horizon colour, aerial perspective and sunsets from one model. See the sky table on page 07.

Day and night

Drive everything from one parameter: sun elevation. The city demo does exactly this. A single sin() of the hour feeds:

  • Sky and fog colour: blend day, dusk and night colours (or move the Sky add-on's sun).
  • Sun: direction, intensity and colour (white at noon, orange near the horizon, off below it).
  • Ambient: the hemisphere light dims but never goes to zero (moonlight and sky glow).
  • Environment map: re-bake it with PMREMGenerator when the sky changes. That is expensive, so only do it when the sun moves noticeably. Verified (official ocean example)
  • Emissive: windows, street lamps, neon and car lights fade in as the sun sets.
  • Exposure and bloom: a camera adapts; bloom grows at night.

Stars, a moon and a light-pollution glow at the horizon finish the night look. The night-street brief asks for a "deep dark blue-orange city sky from light pollution" rather than black. Verified

Vegetation

  • Trees and bushes at scale: instanced low-poly models, or billboards (camera-facing cards) and impostors (pre-rendered views) for distance. Free CC0 tree models: Quaternius, KayKit, Poly Haven (page 10).
  • Grass: thousands of instanced blades with wind in the vertex shader (scrolling noise bends the tip more than the base). The reference is Ghost of Tsushima's GDC 2021 talk (Eric Wohllaib, video):
    • each blade is a cubic Bézier curve, with 15 vertices at high LOD and 7 at low;
    • compute shaders generate the blades and indirect draws render them. Verified
    The browser version: an InstancedMesh of blade strips, or the WebGPU compute path.
  • Scattering: place by noise, slope and altitude; avoid roads and building footprints. The official webgl_instancing_scatter example scatters on a surface.
  • Watch overdraw: alpha-tested leaves are expensive (page 05).

Crowds and traffic

  • Traffic: cars follow lane splines or grid lines, with a speed per car and simple "don't hit the car ahead" spacing. At night, render only the lights (the city demo does this). Traffic lights cycling through phases add life cheaply.
  • Pedestrians and flocks: Reynolds' boids (SIGGRAPH 1987) and his steering behaviours (GDC 1999: seek, flee, arrival, path following, flow fields, flocking = separation + alignment + cohesion). Verified GPU versions: webgl_gpgpu_birds.
  • Animated people: skinned glTF characters are heavy. Crowds use instanced low-poly figures with a shader-driven walk cycle, or vertex-animation textures. Mixamo animations forbid AI/ML training use. Check the licence (page 10).
  • Unreal's City Sample uses its Mass AI framework for crowds and traffic, which is far beyond what a web demo does. Verified

Three.js's CatmullRomCurve3 defaults to the centripetal variant, which is proven never to form cusps or self-intersections within a segment (Yuksel, Schaefer and Keyser 2011). Verified Use getPointAt (arc-length) rather than getPoint, or speed varies along the curve. For a filmic feel, ease in and out, keep the horizon level, aim the camera slightly ahead and down, and avoid pitch changes faster than the eye can follow.

How to read a demo's cameraA perfectly smooth glide that never stops, never looks backwards and never lets the viewer take over is a strong sign of a scripted path. That is completely legitimate, but it hides a lot. Ask for the live link and try to walk off the path. Inference
