Part V — Doing it

Five directions for our next discussion

This guide maps the field. Each direction below goes deeper in one way. They are ordered from "most likely to give you something impressive soon" to "most strategic". For each: what we would do, what you would get, what it costs, and the open questions this research could not settle.

1. Build one scene to "impressive", together

What: pick one of the three scenes (my suggestion is the ocean, the shortest path to photoreal) and push it up the quality ladder with an agent. We would record every prompt, turn, cost and screenshot.

You get: a real scene, and an honest measurement of the time and money "impressive" takes on your hardware, which this research could only estimate from other people's reports.

Open questions: where diminishing returns start; whether a Gerstner ocean or an FFT one on WebGPU is worth the extra work.

2. Our own small benchmark: the same brief, several agents

What: run the starter prompts through two or three agents (for example Claude Code, Codex and Cursor), using the same rules file, and compare blind.

You get: evidence for your own tooling choice instead of leaderboards. AA-3D's top four are statistically tied, and their sandbox differs from your setup.

Open questions: how to score fairly (blind votes, FPS, cost, number of human interventions); whether skills or rules files help (no controlled study exists, see page 11).

3. Agent harness: give the agent better eyes

What: turn this site's QA script into a reusable harness:

  • fixed camera presets;
  • console and FPS gates;
  • a contact sheet of screenshots;
  • an optional critic subagent;
  • deterministic video capture.

Package it as a skill or an MCP tool.

You get: the part that the evidence most strongly links to quality, since early visual checks track rank closely in AA-3D. It is reusable across every 3D project.

Open questions: headless WebGPU on Windows; the right method to time frames (readPixels vs gl.finish, where sources conflict).

4. Real places: a fly-over of a real city

What: use 3D tiles (Google Photorealistic, or Cesium ion's open data) with 3DTilesRendererJS or CesiumJS, with an agent writing the camera path and overlays.

You get: the most "wow per hour" of all routes, but with licence strings attached: no caching, required attribution, and promotional videos of at most 30 s.

Open questions: current Google pricing above the free cap (only a third-party source this time); whether your use is commercial; offline needs.

5. Engine route: Unreal or Godot with MCP

What: try Epic's experimental Unreal MCP (or Godot plus an MCP server) on a small street scene, in a throwaway project with security precautions.

You get: a feel for the high-fidelity ceiling (Lumen, Nanite, PCG). Compare that against how much harder it is for the agent to see and test its results there.

Open questions: hardware (32 GB RAM, 8–16 GB VRAM as a floor); how mature the MCP servers are; the security model (no authentication on Epic's server; the Blender MCP CVE).

Gaps in this research worth closing first

  • No demo from page 12 was re-run; "Verified" means the artefacts are public, not that results were reproduced.
  • No measured FPS for the same scene on integrated, discrete and mobile GPUs.
  • No current subscription prices for coding agents.
  • Headless WebGPU capture and Safari / Firefox behaviour from file:// were not tested; this site was only tested in Chromium (Edge).
